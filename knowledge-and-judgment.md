# Knowledge and Judgment

The epistemic problem is not "how to obtain the truth once and for all." It is how a finite system can form usable judgments when information, time, and the candidate space are incomplete while preserving the ability to revise those judgments.

## 1. Reality, representation, and the current boundary

Differences in reality must be separated from distinctions represented inside a system.

A current model covers only part of reality. Its current boundary marks where the system temporarily stops making finer distinctions; outside that boundary there may still be candidates that have not been acquired, generated, or expressed.

Representations can be organized progressively as:

`distinction → factors → relations and organization → structure → current direction or judgment`

A stable representation is a working structure, not reality itself.

## 2. Finite intelligence is callable structure

Finite intelligence relies on limited resources to form, acquire, invoke, and revise structures.

Capability can be distributed across bodies, memory, tools, models, databases, institutions, and collaborators. Information that is stored but cannot currently be acquired and used reliably does not count as currently callable capability.

A system can also misjudge its own capabilities. Self-model, actual capability, permission, and responsibility must remain distinct.

## 3. Two kinds of unknowns

At minimum, distinguish:

- **unknowns within the model**: variables and structure are defined, but some values are missing;
- **unknowns in the candidate space**: the variables, boundaries, classifications, mechanisms, scales, or candidates themselves have not yet appeared.

"Unknown unknowns" arise mainly from the second category.

A candidate that was not generated, not found by search, or pruned cannot simply be recorded as "impossible."

## 4. Candidate frontier and structural tension

A system can first discover that its current structure is insufficient and only then search for an alternative.

Common signals include:

- persistent residuals;
- counterexamples;
- failed boundary conditions;
- cross-context mismatch;
- new situations outside the original sample range or a changed data distribution;
- the same facts producing incompatible predictions under different structures.

In such cases the system can narrow the scope of a conclusion, reduce its strength, preserve an unknown, or generate new candidates without forcing an immediate replacement answer.

## 5. Acquisition errors and distinction errors are different

Failure can occur at different locations:

- an important signal was not acquired;
- the signal was acquired but distinguished incorrectly;
- the evidence material is genuine but its support strength is overestimated;
- correlation is treated as causation;
- a local regularity is extrapolated beyond its boundary.

Evidence material and evidential support must be separated: possessing one piece of evidence does not mean it is sufficient to support the current conclusion.

## 6. Verification should actively seek discriminating information

Verification should not only look for confirming examples. It should also examine:

- negative and boundary cases;
- competing explanations;
- whether new situations fall outside the original applicability range;
- whether the data distribution has changed;
- causal direction;
- whether residuals have structure;
- which observation would best discriminate among current candidates.

Counterfactuals can help generate tests and explanations, but a counterfactual is not itself evidence about reality.

## 7. Epistemic action does not automatically gain real-world authority

To reduce uncertainty, a system may query, experiment, sample, or request additional information.

But information value is not action permission. Even a highly discriminating experiment still needs independent action qualification when it implicates privacy, safety, rights, cost, or irreversible impact.

## 8. Search, stopping, and revision

Search should compare the additional discriminating value of continued search with its cost.

Stopping can be reasonable, but at minimum preserve:

- the current scope;
- key evidence;
- major assumptions;
- unresolved unknowns;
- rejected candidates that may deserve reconsideration;
- conditions for review and revalidation.

Revision should prefer the shallowest change sufficient to explain new evidence. Without discriminating evidence, a system should not rewrite higher-level structures merely to appear "deeper."

## 9. Judgment and epistemic closure

A judgment is the provisional adoption of a representation or direction under the current purpose, scope, and evidence.

It is not:

- permanent truth;
- a value commitment;
- an organizational decision;
- action authorization.

Epistemic closure means "sufficient for the current purpose to continue or hand off." It does not mean the candidate space is complete.

A judgment that can be handed off should at least carry its scope, evidence, assumptions, unknowns, major alternative explanations, applicability conditions, and review conditions.

## 10. Correction and learning are different

Correcting one error does not mean the system has learned a transferable structure.

What gets consolidated after learning should preserve:

- conditions under which the conclusion holds;
- failure conditions;
- boundaries;
- rejected candidates;
- low-information signals;
- reassessment triggers.

Otherwise the system only remembers the conclusion, not why the conclusion was justified.

## 11. Generating a candidate does not create qualification for judgment

A reasoner or model generating a candidate establishes only that the candidate was generated.

It does not automatically establish that:

- the candidate space is sufficiently complete;
- the current candidate is better than competing candidates;
- search may stop;
- the judgment may enter action.

Each of those requires its own qualification conditions.

## 12. Functional performance does not imply subjective experience

Complex behavior, self-models, memory, language, reward, self-maintenance, learning, strategy, and goal tracking can demonstrate functional capabilities, but none of them alone proves first-person experience.

Whether subjective experience exists can remain unknown or contested. If a mistaken judgment could cause irreversible suffering or other experiential harm, precautionary measures may be justified; precaution itself does not prove that subjective experience exists.
