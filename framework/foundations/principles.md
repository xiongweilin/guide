# General Methodological Principles: Problem Orientation and Proportionate Tests

English | [简体中文](principles.zh-CN.md)

## 1. Scope of generality and necessity

guide addresses diverse questions about understanding, knowledge, evaluation, choice, and action. Its purpose is to reduce confusions and omissions material to a judgment—not to explain all objects through one theory.

- **General** means callable across domains, not identical applicability conditions.
- **Necessary** is problem-relative: address a distinction, relation, or evidential gap if overlooking it could materially change the conclusion. No fixed inventory is thereby proved logically minimal, irreducible, or complete.
- **Proportionate** means tailoring depth to claim type, impact, risk, reversibility, and available evidence. Low-risk judgments do not universally require full audit trails.

## 2. Four operating rules

1. **Anchor the problem:** specify the question, object, scope, time scale, and claim; distinguish cases, populations, and general patterns.
2. **Check relevance:** explore distinctions and systemic links, then ask whether they change understanding, inference, evaluation, or action. Do not enumerate every real difference.
3. **Match standards:** state what defines a distinction, the kind of relation, and the claim type. Valid inference, true premises, empirical effectiveness, and normative legitimacy are not interchangeable.
4. **Revise with counterexamples:** examine strong alternatives, disconfirming evidence, boundary cases, and failure conditions; narrow, downgrade, merge, reopen, or discard claims as warranted.

These rules operate throughout inquiry; they are not merely a final checklist.

## 3. Claim types and standards

| Claim | Main tests | Does not automatically establish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Conceptual/classificatory | Definitions, referents, counterexamples, discriminative use | Real-world causality |
| Formal/logical | Premises, valid derivations, proofs, countermodels | Reality of premises |
| Empirical description | Reliable observations, measurement, sources, and population | Causality or inevitable future |
| Empirical forecast | Precommitted target, horizon, baseline, independent outcome | Causation or authorization |
| Explanation/causality | Alternatives, mechanisms, comparisons, identification and transport assumptions | An available intervention |
| Intervention/action effects | Real conditions, appropriate attribution, external verification | Normative legitimacy or authority |
| Value/normative | Reasons, rights, duties, procedure, authority, trade-offs | Deduction from facts or popularity |

Observation is not infallible fact; evidential support is not certain truth; valid reasoning does not make premises true. Mixed claims require separate checks of their empirical, formal, and normative parts.

## 4. Scope of use

The [nine distinctions](distinctions.md) and [system perspectives](systems.md) are non-exhaustive resources and may correct each other. They are not mandatory exhaustive lists. A relation does not automatically establish causation; self-reference, feedback, emergence, and complex mechanisms require relevant conditions.

For a specified next transition, use [sufficiency](../methods/sufficiency.md) when useful; actual execution additionally requires authority, capability, effect verification, and recovery. Formal proofs, tests, controlled experiments, and long-term outcomes are distinct evidential levels.
