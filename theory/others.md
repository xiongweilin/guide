# Others

[English](./others.md) | [简体中文](./others.zh-CN.md)

> Inspiration: the tension between alignment and conflict.

Others asks: **who affects whom, and in which dimensions are they aligned or in conflict?**

Human activity is not an isolated internal process. Individuals, families, teams, organizations, institutions, and technical systems can change one another's reality, information, costs, values, capabilities, and reachable paths.

The others dimension does not assume that participants share one complete model of the situation, one value scale, or one institutionally recognized authority.

## Minimal coordinates

`relation strength × alignment / conflict`

### Relation strength

Relation strength asks:

> How much does one participant's state, choice, or action change another participant's reality, information, cost, value, capability, or reachable path?

Strong relation does not imply alignment. Weak relation does not imply no relation.

### Alignment / conflict

Alignment and conflict must be located in a dimension rather than applied as one total label to the whole relationship.

Participants may have:

- aligned factual judgments but conflicting values;
- aligned values but conflicting methods;
- aligned goals but competition for resources;
- short-term alignment and long-term conflict;
- different distinction schemes while still having compatible actions.

Social relations therefore cannot be reduced to a simple cooperation/opposition binary.

## Strategic actors and local worlds

A **strategic actor** is a participant whose judgment or action can materially change another participant's later conditions: reality, information, judgment, costs, or reachable paths.

This is an analytical role only.

`strategic actor != subjectivity != responsibility != authority`

Being able to influence others does not by itself establish subjective experience, moral standing, decision rights, or legitimacy.

Different participants also do not automatically share the same distinguished world. They may differ in:

- acquired information;
- distinctions and classifications;
- question structure;
- callable evidence;
- time scale;
- values and goals;
- perceived actors, actions, constraints, and consequences.

Coordination sometimes requires exchanging information, sometimes rebuilding shared distinctions, and sometimes accepting value conflicts that cannot be eliminated.

## Typed power topology

Relation strength says how much participants affect one another; it does not identify which control position they occupy.

Keep the three core powers distinct:

- **definition power**: shaping the problem boundary, candidate space, standards, or agenda;
- **judgment power**: deciding whether evidence or conditions count as sufficient;
- **decision power**: turning judgment into commitment, allocation, permission, or binding choice.

Execution, verification, resource, information, exit, stop, or reopen power should be represented separately only when those differences materially change the analysis.

So:

`relation strength != power type`

`formal role != effective control`

`power to decide != power to define the available options`

`power under current rules != power to change the rules`

Power concentration matters especially when one participant or failure domain controls several positions that are supposed to check one another.

## No pre-given complete game

Strategic uncertainty is not limited to unknown values inside an already defined model.

The unknown may instead concern:

- which actors matter;
- which actions are actually available;
- what information is visible to whom;
- how another participant distinguishes and judges;
- what time structure matters;
- which local descriptions can be combined;
- whether the current interaction has been framed correctly at all.

`unknown variable value != unknown strategic problem structure`

Formal game models, optimization models, or simulations are downstream tools. Their fields should not be filled with invented precision merely because the solver requires a complete object.

## Strategic situation representations and models of others

A participant can hold a **strategic situation representation**: a callable working structure about actors, roles, relations, action conditions, information differences, constraints, likely reactions, time, and consequence paths.

Different representations are an allowed state:

`G_i != G_j`

A **model of another actor** is one participant's working representation of another participant's relevant capability, resources, orientation, authority, information, possible actions, and judgment process.

`model_i(other_j) != other_j's real internal state`

Such a model is also not automatically a probability distribution or a personality type. Those are optional formalizations when the domain supports them.

Finite higher-order modeling is permitted — “I think that you think...” — but depth should increase only when another layer could materially change action candidates, risk, or discriminating information. Failure to model an infinite hierarchy is not failure to understand anything useful.

## Collective action and organizational actors

Aligned interests do not automatically produce collective action:

`aligned interests != coordination != sustained collective capability`

Coordination can depend on thresholds, incentives, trust, communication, institutions, and network position. Likewise, an organization should not be treated as a large individual when internal coalitions, incentives, authority, or information differences materially affect the result.

Keep:

`organization action != unified member preference`

`formal organization goal != actual internal incentive structure`

`formal authority != effective internal control`

`many capable individuals != capable collective`

Internal organizational structure should be reopened only when it changes prediction, responsibility, capability, or legitimacy.

## Networks, structural positions, and identity

Pairwise relations do not exhaust multi-participant structure:

`sum of bilateral relations != network structure`

Network position can change information access, opportunities, bargaining power, dependence, diffusion, exposure, and reachable paths. A **structural position** is any relatively persistent location in a relation structure that materially changes opportunities, costs, risks, or control; the domain may describe it in terms of resources, authority, network position, status, dependency, risk-bearing, exit, class, hierarchy, or market position.

Identity can also occupy different semantic positions:

`self identity != classification by others != institutionally recognized status`

These can affect values, how others judge the participant, and which opportunities institutions make available. Identity therefore often lies across Distinction, Value, and Others rather than belonging to one dimension alone.

## Action, signal, and evidence

Behavior is not automatically a signal, and a signal is not automatically an evidence conclusion.

`action != signal != evidence conclusion`

The same offer, silence, delay, refusal, cooperation, or public statement can:

- fail to be observed;
- be distinguished differently by different observers;
- support competing interpretations;
- change meaning under different institutional or relational expectations.

Silence therefore does not automatically mean consent, refusal, ignorance, or strategy.

When interpretation fails, distinguish whether the problem lies in reality input, acquisition, distinction, signal formation, evidence force, the model of the other actor, or the broader strategic representation.

## Strategic epistemic action and reflexivity

Some actions are primarily **epistemic**: probes, staged disclosure, questions, reversible trials, or bounded experiments can help discriminate between competing models of another participant or of the situation.

Information value does not create action permission. Privacy, safety, rights, cost, and irreversible effects still require independent qualification and authority.

Strategic systems are also reflexive:

`model → judgment → action → changed reality → others adapt → changed reality again`

Forecasts, metrics, incentives, published rules, and revealed strategies can change the behavior being modeled. A relationship that later fails may have been wrong from the beginning, or may have become invalid because the model entered the world and changed the conditions.

## Formal representation and heterogeneous orientations

A joint strategic representation does not imply shared values or goals:

`joint strategic representation != shared values or goals`

`comparable outcomes != common value scale`

Payoff, utility, ranking, and cost are modeling choices. Formal optimality, equilibrium, strategic advantage, or aggregate payoff does not by itself create authority or legitimacy.

## Authority, recognition, and legitimacy

Multi-participant activity should keep separate:

- **formal authority**: who has decision rights under the current institution and scope;
- **recognition**: which participants or institutional structures actually accept an identity, commitment, or authority as a basis for coordination and responsibility expectations;
- **legitimacy**: whether that authority, recognition, or rule has sufficient normative basis under the relevant rights, procedures, and institutional conditions.

`strategic advantage != formal authority != recognition != legitimacy`

Recognition is relational, not a global Boolean. It has a recognizer, an object, a scope, conditions, and a basis. One group may recognize an authority while another does not. Legitimate authority may lack effective recognition; widely recognized authority may still face legitimacy objections.

None of these distinctions automatically proves factual correctness, superior capability, or moral worth.

## Institutions

Institutions do not need to eliminate difference. Their role is to provide inspectable mechanisms for high-relation, potentially conflicting activity:

- problem framing;
- decision procedure;
- authority and representation boundaries;
- commitment and handoff;
- review and appeal;
- exit and renegotiation;
- correction and reopening.

A nominal choice or exit path is not automatically a real one. Whether participants can refuse, appeal, leave, or renegotiate can depend on resources, information, safety, relationships, time, and institutional alternatives.

A durable institution supports stable cooperation while retaining the ability to reopen arrangements when facts, values, authority, recognition, costs, or relationship structures materially change.

## Institutional reproduction and meta-power

Institutions do more than provide procedures. Existing rules and structures can reshape incentives, resources, capabilities, network positions, and bargaining power in ways that help reproduce the institution itself.

A common loop is:

`institution → incentives / constraints → adaptation → resource and capability distribution → future bargaining power → institution reproduction`

Therefore:

`institution persists != institution remains normatively justified`

`institution persists != participants actively chose it again`

Path dependence, switching cost, sunk investment, network effects, organizational routines, legal commitments, and unequal exit capability can all make an arrangement durable even after its original basis weakens.

Political and organizational analysis also needs **meta-power**: control over what reaches visible decision at all.

Keep separate:

`decision power != agenda-setting power`

`choice among offered options != power to define the option set`

`winning under current rules != power to shape the rules, venue, or enforcement conditions`

A system can therefore appear procedurally open at the decision stage while remaining highly concentrated earlier in problem definition, candidate generation, standard setting, resource allocation, or access to review.

The guide does not adopt one universal class, institutional, or political-economy theory. It requires these structural mechanisms to remain representable when they materially change participants' possibility spaces.

## Strategic failure and reopening

When prediction or coordination fails, do not assume that only a parameter needs updating. Failure may come from missing actors, actions, constraints, or time scales; an inadequate model of others; weak evidence; reflexive adaptation; or changes in environment, authority, value, or relationship structure.

Useful strategic memory should preserve enough of the situation, important other-actor models, signals, rejected alternatives, adaptation effects, and reopening conditions to distinguish these possibilities.

## Boundary with the other dimensions

Alignment and conflict among multiple participants can occur in [distinction](./distinction.md), [qualification](./qualification.md), [value](./value.md), [capability](./capability.md), or [change](./change.md). The others dimension describes how those differences become coupled through relationships.

It does not replace those dimensions: a model of another participant is not the participant; strategic influence is not authority; recognition is not legitimacy; and a formally solvable interaction is not automatically qualified for action.
