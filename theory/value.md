# Value

[English](./value.md) | [简体中文](./value.zh-CN.md)

> Inspiration: the tension between what one wants to do and what one ought to do.

Value asks: **what is worth pursuing, avoiding, maintaining, committing to, or refusing?**

Facts describe what is. Value gives direction. Knowing how the world is does not automatically determine where activity should go.

Value formation should not be compressed into one jump from fact, reward, preference, or experience to normativity. Different layers require different evidence and qualification.

## Basic tension

`external norms ↔ internal norms`

- **External norms**: requirements or expectations from family, culture, education, law, institutions, roles, organizations, markets, groups, and relationships.
- **Internal norms**: directions arising from experience, preference, identity, reflection, active choice, and commitment.

This is not a simple binary. External norms can be internalized until their source is no longer noticed; internal norms can change through experience and reflection.

Keep separate:

- where a norm came from;
- whether the person recognizes its source;
- whether it is merely habit, pressure, or regulation;
- whether it has reflective endorsement;
- whether it has become a durable commitment;
- whether it can bind others;
- whether public legitimacy is required.

“I feel this is my value” does not by itself prove independence from external norms.

## Value formation: four layers

A useful non-substitution sequence is:

`consequence difference → system relevance → regulatory polarity → experience-status gate → experienced valence → first-order orientation → reflective endorsement → individual value commitment`

Each arrow introduces a new question. None is automatic.

### Descriptive / functional

A **consequence difference** means that different states, relations, or actions lead to distinguishable downstream conditions. It does not say which side is better.

**System relevance** means that a difference changes the current system's state, capability, relations, resources, reachable paths, maintenance, or recovery conditions. Relevance must be scoped by system boundary, time scale, and conditions.

**Regulatory polarity** appears when such differences enter feedback, comparison, control, judgment, or action organization and produce relatively stable but revisable approach, avoidance, maintenance, recovery, inhibition, or search directions.

A system can therefore regulate toward or away from states through habit, reward, control objectives, or physiological mechanisms without this by itself establishing subjective experience or normative value.

`regulatory polarity != experience != normative conclusion`

### Experiential

Before treating a state as positively or negatively **experienced**, an **experience-status gate** is required: there must be sufficient domain-specific reason to treat the object as a subject with its own experience.

Self-maintenance, reward/loss, goal representation, complex behavior, self-modeling, language reports, learning, adaptation, or strategic ability may all be evidence, but none is automatically sufficient by itself.

The subject-status question may remain `unknown` or `contested`. If the cost of being wrong could involve severe and irreversible welfare harm, that uncertainty may enter action risk without being misreported as proof of subjectivity.

Experienced valence, when qualified, is still not a utility function and does not have to form one common numerical scale.

### Reflective

A **first-order orientation** is a direction that actually enters judgment or action: approach, avoid, preserve, obtain, stop, protect, or commit. It can arise from the body, experience, habit, teaching, relationships, institutions, strategic interaction, or external tasks.

**Reflective endorsement** treats existing orientations, experiences, sources, consequences, and conflicts as objects of judgment and may endorse, revise, suspend, or reject them.

An **individual value commitment** is a current endorsement that can organize later judgment and action while accepting its maintenance conditions, real costs, and revisability.

`revisable != uncommitted`

`committed != exempt from revision`

### Public normative

Individual value does not automatically create authority over others.

When choices affect other people, rights, shared resources, third parties, or institutions, additional public norms, decision procedures, authority, representation, appeal, and legitimacy may be required.

`normatively relevant fact != normative conclusion`

Pain, welfare, fear, attachment, dignity loss, or strong personal commitment can matter greatly without by themselves completing a public normative judgment.

## Source and validity

Values can arise from the body, experience, care and relationships, education, institutions, incentives, habits, path dependence, loss, trauma, imitation, strategic interaction, reflective endorsement, or rejection.

Value-source tracing is useful for understanding how a commitment formed; it is not a search for one hidden “true cause.”

Keep separate:

`explaining a source != proving invalidity`

`natural source != normative justification`

`socialized source != false value`

`personal endorsement != authority over others`

`social acceptance != automatic legitimacy`

Source, endorsement, validity, and public binding force are different questions.

## Manipulation, coercion, and real exit

The important question is not merely whether a value was socially influenced. Most values have social and historical sources.

What matters is the structure of influence:

- systematic deception or information control;
- threat, punishment, or resource control that compresses real choice;
- blocking alternative information, relationships, or exit paths;
- exploitation of dependency, vulnerability, or asymmetric power;
- whether the orientation remains reflectively endorsed after the pressure is removed.

Finding manipulation or coercion does not reveal what the person “really ought to want.” It weakens the basis for treating the surface commitment as freely and durably endorsed.

A nominal exit option is not the same as real exit capability. Exit may require resources, safety, identity, relationships, knowledge, time, and institutional conditions.

## Plural values and comparability

Multiple values can be jointly represented without being reducible to one common scale.

`comparable outcomes != common value scale`

Some contexts support cost, preference, or utility representations. Others need to preserve rights boundaries, non-compensable losses, role obligations, identity commitments, or values that remain only partially comparable.

A more stable equilibrium or a larger aggregate payoff therefore does not by itself settle what should be accepted.

## Commitment and reopening

Commitment does not permanently freeze value.

Reopening is appropriate when persistent conflict or changed conditions materially weaken the old basis, including:

- sustained conflict between experience, first-order orientation, and reflective endorsement;
- commitments that cannot be maintained together;
- a factual basis that has been materially falsified;
- repeated externalized harm or unanticipated loss;
- changed body, relationship, environment, or identity conditions;
- coercion, manipulation, severe dependency, or lack of real exit.

Reopening a value judgment does not reveal one hidden “true value.” It means only that the old qualification can no longer be assumed to continue.

Long duration is not permanent exemption from review, and temporary negative experience does not automatically invalidate a long-term commitment.

## Boundary with the other dimensions

- The objects and norm sources involved in value must first be [distinguished](./distinction.md).
- A value entering commitment, rule, or action needs independent [qualification](./qualification.md).
- “Worth doing” does not imply current [capability](./capability.md), including real exit capability.
- Values can change with experience and structure [change](./change.md).
- When values affect multiple people, [others](./others.md) introduces alignment, conflict, strategic interaction, authority, recognition, and legitimacy.
